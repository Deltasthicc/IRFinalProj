# 11. Conclusions and limitations

## Conclusions

TradeMesh combines a search engine built from scratch with a verified question-answering layer. The query side detects misspelt company names with a bigram shortlist and Levenshtein confirmation (39 of 40 typo'd names resolved correctly in our test) and uses the result to filter retrieval and to split comparison questions. The answer side gives a language model five labelled passages, requires citations, and checks them with a deterministic verifier that flags invented citations and numbers that do not occur in the cited text; the system abstains when the model says the evidence is insufficient. On 20 test questions rated blind by one human, answers built on hybrid retrieval with reranking scored 1.20 on a 0 to 2 scale, against 0.70 for closed-book (difference +0.50, 95% interval +0.20 to +0.80) and 0.80 for BM25-based RAG (best-RAG minus BM25-RAG +0.40, interval +0.20 to +0.60). Best-RAG was reliably better than both; BM25-based RAG was not reliably better than closed-book (+0.10, interval -0.15 to +0.35), and it was worse on topical questions because it abstained on three of seven. A first, model-assisted rating of the same answers gave the same order and the same three verdicts at a higher level (1.70, 1.325, 1.175); the human and the model agree exactly on only 47% to 53% of answers (kappa 0.23 to 0.27), the human being stricter. The best-RAG over BM25-RAG verdict changed when one question (T10) was corrected after the first scoring (see the limitations); with the correction it holds under leave-one-question-out (+0.37 to +0.42). The model's own insufficient-evidence verdict separated all 6 unanswerable test questions from 20 answerable ones, while a threshold on the retrieval score did not (precision 0.385, recall 0.833), so the final rule is the model's verdict. Retrieval quality (nDCG@5) did not predict the answer rating (Spearman +0.00 with human ratings, +0.007 with model ratings, n = 40, interval about plus or minus 0.3). The human ratings come from a single rater who belongs to the project; the relevance judgments behind the retrieval results are still model-assisted, with no human validation.

## Limitations

* **Small, hand-made test sets.** The entity test has 40 typos written by us, the RAG study has 20 questions (6 to 7 per question type) and the abstention test has 6 unanswerable test questions, all clearly outside the corpus. We report paired bootstrap intervals and call a difference reliable only when its interval excludes 0; per-type numbers are descriptive. A perfect 6 of 6 on abstention has a 95% lower bound of about 0.54.
* **One human rater, who is not independent.** The headline answer ratings and citation checks come from a single project member, blind to the system except that closed-book answers can be recognised by having no citations, so no inter-human agreement was measured and the ratings may carry that person's preferences. The reference answers they wrote averaged 85 words (the plan was one to two sentences), which probably made "complete" a high bar: 46 of 60 ratings are a 1.
* **A reference was corrected after the first scoring.** For T10 the rater's first reference said that the filings contain no answer; we then found that Amazon's and Microsoft's own 10-Ks do answer it, and the rater rewrote the reference and re-rated that question's three answers. The correction changed best-RAG minus BM25-RAG from +0.25 (interval -0.10 to +0.55) to +0.40 (interval +0.20 to +0.60), so the earlier verdict on that comparison hinged on one judgment; with the corrected rating it holds under leave-one-question-out (+0.37 to +0.42), and the other two comparisons barely moved. No other rating was changed. Other references were not re-checked against the full filings, so a similar error elsewhere cannot be ruled out.
* **Model-assisted ratings and judgments remain, and disagree with the human ones.** Answers were first rated by one language model in two passes; those ratings are kept for comparison but agree with the human on only 47% to 53% of answers (kappa 0.23 to 0.27), so they should not be cited as validated. The retrieval relevance judgments are model-assisted and were not validated by a human (the optional human validation sample was not done). The audit trail for the model-assisted ratings is in `docs/report_C/audit/`; the human protocol and sheets are in `eval/human/`.
* **One small language model, one encoder.** All answers come from `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, chosen because the free tier of larger models allows about 20 requests a day. Conclusions may not transfer to stronger models. Cached answers fix the demo, but they are tied to this model version.
* **Strict number matching.** The verifier requires a number to appear exactly (after unit normalisation). A correct but rounded or recomputed figure is flagged, and a number that is present but attached to the wrong claim is not caught. The verifier checks *presence*, not *entailment*; a citation can be real yet not support the sentence, which is why citation precision is judged separately (by LLM passes). The verifier flagged none of the 55 answered outputs on dev and test after a sentence-splitting bug was fixed, so on this data it did not demonstrate catching an error; and it cannot catch an omission such as the missing segment in question R09.
* **The retrieval score is not a usable confidence.** The score rule uses the hybrid retriever's score before reranking divided by query length. Rank fusion makes that score depend on rank positions, so unanswerable and answerable questions overlap (the 1995 Pfizer revenue question tied for the highest confidence of all 40 items). τ tuned on dev came out as 0, which turns the rule off, so abstention here is the model's own verdict. We saw the score-only numbers on the test items before choosing the verdict rule, so that choice was not fixed in advance. We did not test the cross-encoder score as a confidence.
* **Entity matcher.** Plain Levenshtein counts a transposition as two edits, so a swapped-letter ticker such as `MSTF` is missed. Names unknown to the dictionary are never detected, and a corpus word that is also a company alias can be ignored or accepted wrongly. Possessive forms of company names are also handled inconsistently: a form such as "Microsoft's" is skipped when it already occurs as a word in the filings ("Tesla's" is recognised, "Microsoft's" and "Apple's" are not), so 3 of the 16 company-naming needs (R06 on dev, R07 and R09 on test) and 3 of the 10 unanswerable questions ran without the company filter. We found this after the study and did not change the code, so that the reported results stay reproducible; it may have contributed to the weak results on the table questions R07 and R09.
* **Hand-set section prior.** The weights g(Item) were set by judgement, not learned. λ was tuned on the 10 dev needs only and gave at most +0.019 nDCG@10 there (no demonstrated benefit); it is 0 in all reported test runs, so the section prior is not part of the reported results and was never evaluated on the test needs.
* **Scope.** English, US filings, the latest 10-K of about 20 large companies, text and tables only (charts only through the gated vision track). Questions about trends over several years are out of scope.
* **Not a financial product.** The system makes no investment claims; answers are only as good as the retrieved passages and must be checked against the filing.
